A field guide in five parts

Learn about our coasts

Coastal water is connected to the land, wildlife, and communities around it. Explore the sections below, then try a fresh set of questions in the quiz.

01 / CONNECTED WATER

Coasts begin far inland

A coast is more than the line where waves meet sand. Bays, estuaries, tidal flats, rocky shores, dunes, wetlands, and nearshore waters are all part of a changing coastal system. Rivers carry freshwater toward the sea, tides push saltwater inland, and winds and waves move water and sediment along shorelines.

Follow the watershed

A watershed is the land area that drains rain and snowmelt into creeks, rivers, bays, or the ocean. A storm drain near a school can connect to a stream, then a river, and eventually a coastal bay. That connection means choices made far from a beach can still affect coastal water.

Where fresh and salt water meet

An estuary is a place where rivers meet the sea and fresh and salt water mix. Changing tides and water conditions create a range of habitats. Estuaries are often called nurseries because many fish and other animals find food, shelter, or places to grow there.

02 / HABITATS & COMMUNITIES

Healthy coasts support life and people

Coastal ecosystems include salt marshes, seagrass beds, kelp forests, oyster reefs, sandy beaches, rocky shorelines, and tidal wetlands. Each has its own mix of plants and animals. Shorebirds feed along beaches and mudflats; young fish use sheltered estuary waters; kelp forests provide underwater habitat.

Natural benefits

Wetland plants and seagrasses can trap some sediment and absorb nutrients as water moves through them. Wetlands also hold and slow some floodwater, which can reduce flooding and erosion in certain places. These benefits depend on local conditions: wetlands do not prevent every flood or storm, and a damaged habitat may not work as well as a healthy one.

People are part of the coast

Coastal waters and estuaries support fishing, shipping, tourism, recreation, and cultural traditions. They are also places where people live and work. Protecting habitat and keeping pollution out of the water helps support wildlife and the communities that depend on healthy coastal places.

03 / POLLUTION PATHWAYS

Plastic and litter can travel

Marine debris is human-made material that ends up in oceans, rivers, lakes, or shorelines. It can come from litter, overflowing or uncovered bins, lost fishing gear, and waste carried by stormwater. Plastic is durable. Sunlight, saltwater, and waves can break it into smaller pieces called microplastics, which are difficult to remove once spread through the environment.

Runoff carries a mix of materials

Rain and snowmelt flow over roofs, roads, parking lots, yards, and fields. Along the way, runoff may pick up soil, pet waste, fertilizer, pesticides, oil, and other pollutants. Pavement prevents water from soaking into the ground, so more water can flow quickly into storm drains and nearby streams.

Nutrients and algae

Nitrogen and phosphorus are natural nutrients, but too much entering a waterway can cause algae to grow faster than the ecosystem can handle. Some blooms reduce light or oxygen in the water; some produce toxins. Not every algae bloom is harmful, and a bloom’s appearance alone cannot confirm whether it is toxic.

Sewage, chemicals, and oil

Heavy rain can overwhelm some sewer systems, and broken pipes or poorly maintained septic systems can also release wastewater. Sewage may carry germs that make people sick. Oil and chemical spills can expose wildlife and habitats to harmful substances. A photo or community report can point out a concern, but it cannot identify a pollutant or prove water is unsafe.

04 / OBSERVE RESPONSIBLY

Notice carefully, then check trusted guidance

Unusual color, floating debris, a sheen, a strong odor, or dead wildlife can be worth reporting. These signs do not tell you exactly what caused them. For example, some natural conditions can change water color, and not all algal blooms are harmful. A report is an observation for awareness, not a lab result or an official beach advisory.

Put safety first

Do not touch unknown liquids, oily material, dead wildlife, or a suspected bloom. Keep children and pets away from discolored water and follow posted signs. If you want to swim, check current local or state beach advisories and health department guidance; Aqua Watch reports do not replace official water testing or safety notices.

Make a useful observation

Choose the closest report category, note the date, and describe only what you can see. A clear description might say “several plastic bottles beside the tide line” rather than guessing where they came from. Avoid including your name, phone number, or other personal details because exact report locations and descriptions are public on this site.

05 / SMALL ACTIONS

Prevention starts with everyday choices

Use a reusable bottle or bag when it works for you, secure trash before windy or rainy weather, and put waste in the correct bin. Recycling rules vary by place, so check local guidance instead of assuming every plastic item belongs in a recycling bin.

Keep pollution out of storm drains

Pick up pet waste, never pour paint or motor oil into a drain, and follow directions when using yard products. Use fertilizer only as directed and avoid applying it before rain. If you live near a stream, native plants can help hold soil in place and provide habitat.

Help together

Join a supervised shoreline or neighborhood cleanup, learn how your community handles stormwater, and share reliable local beach advisories. A cleanup can remove visible litter, while preventing waste from reaching waterways helps reduce what needs to be cleaned up later. Small actions do not solve every source of pollution, but they can be part of broader community efforts.
